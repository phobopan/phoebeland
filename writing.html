On the small museumApril 18, 2026+

Why a personal website should feel more like a wunderkammer than a résumé.

I have been thinking about the difference between a résumé and a cabinet of curiosities. The résumé is a contract: it makes a promise about what you can do, and it lists evidence in support of that promise. The cabinet is something stranger. It admits that you don't know yet what the objects are for. It admits that the value of a thing is sometimes simply that you keep returning to it, that it has, against all professional advice, become part of how you see.

The personal website, in 2026, has mostly become a résumé. We grade them on conversion rate. We compress them into hero / projects / contact. The bones are the same on every site I open: a thin header, a wall of case studies, a CTA at the bottom. They are extremely effective at communicating that someone is a competent worker. They are extremely bad at communicating that someone is alive.

I keep wondering what it would look like to build the other thing. A small museum. A wunderkammer. Not a portfolio, exactly — though it can hold the portfolio inside it — but a room arranged by an actual person according to actual taste. A room that doesn't apologize for including, next to the serious work, the sketch on the napkin, the half-finished tool, the essay nobody read, the song that was playing while you wrote the code.

The cabinet says: here is what I have collected, and the collecting is itself the work.

This is harder than it sounds. The risk of a wunderkammer is that it becomes self-indulgent: a wall of personal effects with no through-line, beautiful to the maker and inscrutable to anyone else. So you have to build it like a curator, not a hoarder. Each thing has to earn its place. The captions have to be honest. The arrangement has to imply a sensibility, even when it isn't explicit.

What I love about the small-museum frame is that it changes what counts as finished. A résumé is finished when every item has a star next to it. A cabinet is finished when the room feels like you. The work of finishing is the work of arranging — pulling some things forward, letting others rest. You can keep a cabinet your whole life. You will never be done with it. That is, I think, the point.

So I'm going to keep building this site that way. A few rooms, a few drawers. Some objects you walk past. Some you stop at. A small museum, quietly kept.
